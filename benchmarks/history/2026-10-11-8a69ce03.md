# Cursor benchmark of PyAthena 4.0.0 on 2026-10-11

This entry records a bounded large-data run of the [benchmark harness](../README.md) at commit [`8a69ce03`](https://github.com/pyathena-dev/PyAthena/tree/8a69ce03531ce501e7855b3650ead33d3e493e53/benchmarks).
The `pyathena/` package, `pyproject.toml`, and `uv.lock` at that commit are identical to the `v4.0.0` tag, so the measurements describe PyAthena 4.0.0 as released.
The installed distribution reports the version `4.0.1.dev4+g8a69ce035` because its version is derived from the distance to the latest tag.

## Selection

The run used the bounded selection in [Running on a fleet](../README.md#running-on-a-fleet): 444 jobs over three scales.

- **medium**: every suite (single, concurrent, init), both shapes, five repetitions.
- **large**: arraysize 1000 for every library; arraysize 100 for the non-API libraries and for the synchronous Cursor; concurrent row paging only for the flat Cursor; initialization for S3FSCursor and the DataFrame libraries; five repetitions, three for the two Cursor selections.
- **xlarge**: synchronous API only, both shapes, no DictCursor, DataFrame row output only at arraysize 1000, three repetitions.

The configuration matched `config.toml` except for `chunksizes = [10000, 100000, 1000000]`, `concurrency = [1, 10]`, and `timeout_seconds = 5400.0`.
No warmups ran, because every trial already starts in a fresh process.
The small scale was not measured.

## Run record

All twelve Spot hosts started in `us-west-2a`; the group's attempts to launch in other zones failed with Spot capacity errors (`InsufficientInstanceCapacity` and `UnfulfillableCapacity`).
The group's `AZRebalance` process was suspended before the workers started, so that the group would not replace busy hosts to balance zones.
Workers used `--api-slots 10 --terminate-host`, which limits the fleet to ten simultaneous paging queries, sized for the account's GetQueryResults rate of 100 calls per second.

The queue was published at 04:08 UTC, and the last job of the main queue finished at 07:45 UTC.
Two Spot interruptions occurred, at 06:13 and 07:34 UTC; both replacements started in `us-west-2c` and received a worker by instance ID.
The first interrupted host was idle; the second was running `single-large-nested-dict-aio-csv-rows-a1000`.
That job was retried after all workers had exited, on the kept host and against the same manifest, from 07:55 to 08:18 UTC.

The run recorded 3,116 trials: 3,062 successful and 54 unsupported (Wrangler CSV with the nested shape), with no failed trial.
The median Athena queue time of every case was at most 109 ms.

The run used 44.1 Spot host-hours at about $0.16 per hour, about $7.0 for EC2.
Athena scanned about 43 GB (about $0.35), and EBS volumes and public IPv4 addresses add about $0.4 from the host-hours; S3 request charges were not itemized.

## Findings

1. **The result transport dominated retrieval time at large scales.**
   At 10 million rows, Athena took 16 seconds to write flat results as Parquet through UNLOAD or CTAS and 29 seconds as CSV; for nested results, 31 to 33 seconds as Parquet and 214 seconds as CSV.
   End to end, ArrowCursor and PolarsCursor with `unload=True` returned 10 million flat rows in 18 to 19 seconds and nested rows in 35 to 36 seconds, against 36 to 110 seconds (flat) and 203 to 379 seconds (nested) through CSV.
2. **Chunked reads bounded memory for CSV and for Wrangler's Parquet reads.**
   At 10 million flat rows and a chunksize of 10,000, peak RSS fell from 5,091 to 256 MiB for PandasCursor, from 6,821 to 880 MiB for PolarsCursor, and from 7,682 to 273 MiB for Wrangler CSV.
   Wrangler CTAS and UNLOAD with nested rows fell from about 19,000 MiB to about 690 MiB.
   With chunks, PandasCursor's client result time changed by -4% to +8% and PolarsCursor's fell; chunked Wrangler CSV took 106 to 111 seconds instead of 89.
3. **Chunking did not bound memory for UNLOAD results in PolarsCursor or PandasCursor** (#1131).
   PolarsCursor's peak RSS stayed at about 3,700 MiB (flat) and 6,100 MiB (nested) at 10 million rows for every chunksize, the same as without chunking, while its CSV path fell to 880 to 980 MiB at chunksizes of 10,000 and 100,000.
   PandasCursor reads UNLOAD results whole regardless of chunksize, so its chunked UNLOAD cases were not run.
4. **Nested Parquet results converted to pandas used more than three times the memory of Arrow or Polars.**
   At 10 million nested rows, PandasCursor with `unload=True` peaked at 19,604 MiB and Wrangler CTAS and UNLOAD at about 19,000 MiB, against 5,711 MiB for ArrowCursor and 6,093 MiB for PolarsCursor.
5. **Row paging through GetQueryResults scaled with the number of pages and kept memory flat.**
   Cursor took 213 seconds for 1 million flat rows at arraysize 1000 and 991 seconds at arraysize 100; 10 million rows took 2,141 seconds (flat) and 2,978 seconds (nested) at arraysize 1000, with peak RSS at about 180 MiB.
   DictCursor matched Cursor within 5%.
   For row output from 10 million rows, ArrowCursor with `unload=True` took 27 seconds (flat) and 67 seconds (nested), and S3FSCursor 354 and 1,601 seconds.
6. **PandasCursor matched Wrangler except for unchunked CSV reads** (#1132).
   With `unload=True`, PandasCursor took 18.9 seconds (flat) and 87.5 seconds (nested) for 10 million rows, against 20.4 and 91.6 seconds for Wrangler UNLOAD, with similar memory; chunked CSV reads took about the same time for both.
   Without chunks, PandasCursor read CSV results in 13.7 seconds at 1 million flat rows and 110.5 seconds at 10 million, against 12.4 and 88.9 seconds for Wrangler; Athena's execution time was the same for both.
   At 10 million rows, ArrowCursor and PolarsCursor with `unload=True` took 35 to 36 seconds for nested rows against about 91 seconds for Wrangler CTAS and UNLOAD, and 18 to 19 seconds for flat rows against 18 to 21 seconds.
7. **The synchronous, ThreadPool, and native asyncio APIs performed alike for single queries.**
   The median spread among the three APIs was 1.7% over 128 cases; most larger differences were about one second, the polling interval, and the largest was 1.9 seconds.
   Constructing a result set with `asyncio.to_thread()` took 0.96 to 1.16 times as long as direct construction.
8. **Ten simultaneous queries slowed each query by a factor that depended on the library.**
   At 1 million flat rows, the time per query grew by a factor of about 1.0 for ArrowCursor, 1.7 to 1.8 for PolarsCursor, 2.3 for PandasCursor, 1.9 to 2.0 for Cursor, and 6.5 to 6.7 for S3FSCursor (8.4 to 8.5 for nested rows).
   Athena's execution time (about 5.7 seconds for flat rows) and queue time (about 58 ms) did not change between one and ten queries, so the slowdown arose in the client.
   ThreadPool and native asyncio APIs behaved alike.
   The ten concurrent Cursor queries paged at about 23 to 24 GetQueryResults calls per second in total, below the account's rate of 100.
   During the nested S3FSCursor batch with native asyncio, the host's load average stayed at about 1.0 on 8 vCPUs: the ten queries converted rows on about one core at a time.
   With native asyncio, the event loop lagged by up to 0.81 seconds for S3FSCursor and Cursor rows, and by at most 0.04 seconds for ArrowCursor.
   PolarsCursor created up to 361 threads for ten queries.

## Limits

- The small scale (10,000 rows) was not measured.
- At xlarge, only the synchronous API ran, without DictCursor, with three repetitions; asynchronous behavior at that scale is inferred from 1 million rows.
- Concurrency covered 1 and 10 queries; 50 and 100 were not measured.
- SQLAlchemy and `pandas.read_sql_query()` were not measured; the Cursor row results describe the GetQueryResults paging that they use with the default cursor.
- Large row paging at arraysize 100 and concurrent row paging ran for Cursor only, with three repetitions.
- Three or five trials per case make p95 values descriptive.
- Times include Athena execution and one-second polling; small differences between cases at medium scale often reflect the polling interval.
- Peak RSS is sampled every 50 ms and can miss short peaks.
- Results from other instance types or architectures are not directly comparable.
